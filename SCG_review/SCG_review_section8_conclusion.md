**Section title:**  
Knowledge gaps, future directions, and conclusion

## 8.1 Knowledge gaps

Although the superior cervical ganglion (SCG) has been studied for more than a century, its role in cerebrovascular physiology and disease remains incompletely understood. The preceding sections identify several recurring limitations.

First, much of the anatomical and functional evidence derives from animal models, and the extent to which SCG projection patterns and cerebrovascular effects are conserved in humans is uncertain. The SCG is anatomically variable, and bilateral or accessory sympathetic pathways may partly compensate for unilateral interruption, complicating the interpretation of experimental lesions and clinical blocks.

Second, the molecular heterogeneity of SCG neurons is only beginning to be defined. Single-cell transcriptomic studies show that the SCG contains multiple neuronal subtypes with distinct molecular profiles and target-organ selectivity, but the causal relationship between subtype identity, ion channel phenotype, and cerebrovascular function has not been established. Most disease-related molecular and electrophysiological data come from cardiac or diabetic models, and direct evidence in cerebral ischemia, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or hypertension-induced cerebrovascular disease is limited.

Third, the physiological versus pathological role of SCG activity remains a central unresolved issue. Resting cerebral blood flow is minimally influenced by SCG removal, yet intense SCG activation can produce profound cerebral vasoconstriction and hypoperfusion. It is unclear when SCG activity shifts from a protective restraint on cerebral overperfusion to a harmful driver of hypoperfusion, vasospasm, and vascular remodelling.

Fourth, the clinical evidence for SCG-targeted intervention is still weak. Most human data are case series, technical reports, or extrapolations from stellate ganglion block. Randomised trials comparing SCG block, endovascular transmural blockade, or other SCG-modulating approaches with standard care are lacking.

## 8.2 Future directions

Several directions could substantially advance the field.

In basic research, the SCG should be studied with modern circuit and molecular tools. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic atlases should be linked to retrograde tracing from specific cerebrovascular targets, enabling identification of the SCG subtypes that innervate defined segments of the cerebral circulation. Chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulation of these subtypes, together with in vivo imaging of cerebral blood flow, could establish causal roles for specific SCG populations in autoregulation, ischemic injury, and vasospasm.

In translational models, SCG interventions should be tested across the temporal phases of cerebrovascular disease. It is important to determine whether SCG blockade is beneficial in the hyperacute, subacute, or chronic phase after ischemia, and whether the optimal strategy differs for ischemic stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and hypertensive small-vessel disease. Endovascular transmural SCG blockade is particularly attractive because it offers selective, minimally invasive, and potentially reversible modulation, but its safety, durability, and haemodynamic effects require rigorous evaluation.

The neuroimmune and neuroendocrine functions of the SCG also deserve greater attention. Lymph node-like remodelling, satellite glial activation, and immune-mediated pineal denervation suggest that SCG dysfunction may influence systemic inflammation, sleep, and circadian rhythms after stroke. Studies should test whether preserving or restoring SCG-dependent pineal function improves post-stroke sleep and recovery, and whether SCG neuroimmune signalling can be targeted without compromising sympathetic homeostasis.

Finally, acupuncture and electroacupuncture offer a clinically accessible way to modulate SCG activity. The demonstration that electroacupuncture suppresses paraventricular nucleus–SCG sympathetic overactivation in myocardial infarction provides a template for similar studies in cerebral ischemia. Future work should define the acupuncture parameters, central pathways, and SCG neuronal subtypes involved, and should test whether electroacupuncture improves cerebral perfusion or functional outcome after stroke through SCG-mediated mechanisms.

## 8.3 Conclusion

The SCG is best understood as an integrative autonomic node rather than a simple sympathetic relay. It provides the dominant sympathetic innervation of the proximal cerebral circulation, expresses a diverse molecular and electrophysiological repertoire, participates in neuroimmune and neuroendocrine regulation, and can be modulated by surgical, pharmacological, endovascular, and acupuncture-based approaches. Under pathological conditions, SCG overactivity contributes to cerebral vasoconstriction, hypoperfusion, vasospasm, and vascular remodelling, making it a promising target for cerebrovascular therapy. However, major gaps remain in linking SCG neuronal subtypes to specific vascular functions, defining the transition from protective to pathological sympathetic activity, and generating controlled clinical evidence. A mechanistic framework that integrates SCG anatomy, electrophysiology, cerebrovascular physiology, and neuromodulation will be essential for translating SCG biology into new treatments for stroke and related cerebrovascular diseases.
