# Section 7 — English Draft

**Section title:**  
Neuromodulation targeting the superior cervical ganglion

---

## 7.1 SCG block for pain and headache

The superior cervical ganglion (SCG) has been a target of therapeutic blockade for more than a century, particularly for craniofacial pain and headache. Early clinical experience reported beneficial effects of repeated SCG blocks in patients with migraine, including ocular migraine variants, although these studies were uncontrolled by modern standards (Harder, 1981). More recently, ultrasound-guided SCG block combined with standard triptan therapy improved acute migraine relief compared with triptan alone, supporting the continued use of SCG block as an adjunctive treatment in selected patients (Yue et al., 2025). The rationale for SCG block in headache is based on interrupting sympathetic efferent traffic to cranial vessels and on modulating sensory–sympathetic coupling within the trigeminovascular system.

Acupuncture has also been combined with ganglionic local opioid analgesia in patients with trigeminal neuralgia, indicating that SCG-adjacent interventions may be integrated with neuromodulatory approaches rather than used as isolated procedures (Spacek et al., 1998). Although this older literature is limited, it anticipates current interest in combining peripheral nerve blocks with non-pharmacological neuromodulation.

## 7.2 Surgical denervation and experimental models

Superior cervical ganglionectomy and cervical sympathectomy have been widely used to dissect the contribution of SCG output to vascular and metabolic regulation. Standardised surgical protocols in rats and mice emphasise complete removal of the ganglion, preservation of adjacent structures, and objective confirmation of denervation (Madhani et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). These models have provided much of the mechanistic evidence reviewed in earlier sections, including protection against cerebral ischemia, prevention of cerebral vasospasm, attenuation of hypertension-induced vascular remodelling, and reduction of neurogenic inflammation (Day & Theodosopoulos, 2003; Kim et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025).

Surgical denervation remains a valuable experimental tool, but its clinical translation is limited by irreversibility and the risk of Horner syndrome, ptosis, anhidrosis, and other autonomic disturbances. Therefore, recent efforts have focused on less destructive and more selective ways to modulate SCG activity.

## 7.3 Endovascular and image-guided approaches

Improved anatomical imaging has facilitated more precise SCG targeting. Magnetic resonance imaging can identify the SCG and distinguish it from adjacent vagal ganglia or retropharyngeal lymph nodes, which is important for planning both diagnostic interpretation and image-guided intervention (Yokota et al., 2018). In the interventional neuroradiology setting, a novel endovascular transmural technique has been developed to pharmacologically block the SCG from within the adjacent vascular lumen, and this approach prevented sympathetic-mediated cerebral vasospasm in a preclinical model (Kim et al., 2024). Because the SCG lies in close anatomical relation to the cervical internal carotid artery, endovascular transmural blockade offers a potentially selective and minimally invasive alternative to open ganglionectomy or non-selective cervical sympathetic block.

Direct electrical stimulation of the SCG has also been used experimentally and produces potent cerebral vasoconstriction, confirming that the same structure targeted for blockade can be used to activate the pathway and test its functional role (Kim et al., 2022). Radiofrequency lesioning of the SCG has been reported in selected pain and tinnitus populations, but high-quality controlled data are lacking.

## 7.4 Acupuncture, electroacupuncture, and autonomic neuromodulation

The SCG is increasingly recognised as a relay or target for acupuncture-related autonomic regulation. In mice with myocardial infarction, electroacupuncture at PC6 attenuated sympathetic overactivation along the paraventricular nucleus–SCG axis and improved cardiac function, directly implicating the SCG in the central–peripheral pathway through which acupuncture modulates autonomic output (Peng et al., 2026). This finding is important for two reasons. First, it shows that acupuncture effects are not limited to local or segmental mechanisms but can be transmitted through defined sympathetic relays. Second, it identifies the SCG as a measurable node for studying how somatic stimulation influences visceral sympathetic drive.

The human cervical vagus nerve also contains sympathetic fibres, and the SCG communicates with adjacent cervical nerves, providing anatomical substrates through which vagal, auricular, or cervical somatic stimulation might influence SCG output (Ruigrok et al., 2023). Experimental genetic tools that label peripheral autonomic ganglia, such as Emx1-Cre, may help dissect the specific SCG populations recruited by these neuromodulatory inputs (Ning et al., 2022).

## 7.5 From gross blockade to subtype-specific neuromodulation

A limitation of current SCG-targeted therapies is that they generally inhibit or destroy the entire ganglion, even though SCG neurons are molecularly and functionally heterogeneous. The single-cell and transcriptomic data discussed in Section 3 indicate that distinct SCG subtypes innervate different cranial targets and express different receptors and ion channels. Future neuromodulation should therefore aim to selectively inhibit the SCG populations that drive pathological vasoconstriction or neuroinflammation while sparing those involved in physiological homeostasis. Potential strategies include receptor-selective pharmacological agents, viral vector-mediated chemogenetic or optogenetic manipulation, and image-guided delivery of neuromodulatory drugs to specific regions of the ganglion. These approaches remain experimental but represent a logical next step for cerebrovascular applications.

## 7.6 Summary and knowledge gaps

The SCG is accessible to a broad range of neuromodulatory interventions, including local anaesthetic block, surgical ganglionectomy, endovascular transmural blockade, radiofrequency lesioning, and acupuncture or electroacupuncture. Preclinical studies support a therapeutic role for SCG modulation in cerebral vasospasm, ischemic injury, and sympathetic overactivation, but clinical evidence remains limited and largely uncontrolled. Key gaps include the lack of randomised trials comparing SCG-targeted interventions with standard care, uncertainty about the optimal timing and laterality of intervention, and limited understanding of how acupuncture or vagal stimulation recruits specific SCG pathways. Addressing these gaps will determine whether SCG neuromodulation can move from an experimental strategy to a clinically useful treatment for cerebrovascular disease.
