# Section 4 — English Draft

**Section title:**  
Regulation of cerebral blood flow and vascular tone by the superior cervical ganglion

---

## 4.1 Effector pathways for cerebral vasomotor control

The superior cervical ganglion (SCG) projects to the proximal cerebral arteries through the internal carotid plexus, with the highest density of sympathetic nerve terminals on large vessels of the circle of Willis and progressively lower density in more distal pial branches. These fibres release norepinephrine and co-transmitters that act on vascular smooth muscle to produce vasoconstriction, while endothelial and perivascular mechanisms provide counterbalancing vasodilator signals (Goadsby, 2013). In addition to noradrenergic transmission, neuropeptides present in perivascular nerves, including neuropeptide Y and others, can exert prolonged vascular actions that are not captured by acute pharmacological blockade of adrenoceptors (Uddman & Edvinsson, 1989). These anatomical and neurochemical features indicate that SCG-dependent cerebrovascular control is distributed along the arterial tree but weighted toward the proximal, high-resistance segments.

## 4.2 SCG activation and cerebral vasoconstriction

Direct evidence that SCG activation constricts cerebral vessels comes from electrical stimulation studies. In swine, superior cervical ganglion stimulation produced potent cerebral vasoconstriction, and stimulation of the cervical sympathetic chain induced marked cerebral hypoperfusion, demonstrating that intense sympathetic activation can overcome local autoregulatory mechanisms and reduce brain perfusion (Kim et al., 2022a; Kim et al., 2022b). These effects are consistent with the notion that the SCG is a powerful vasoconstrictor pathway when activated under conditions of high sympathetic drive, even though its influence may be modest at rest.

The constrictor response is not purely α-adrenergic. Sympathetic and nitrergic mechanisms interact within the cerebral vascular wall, and L-type calcium channels have been implicated in sympathetic α3β2-nicotinic receptor-mediated cerebral nitrergic vasodilation, suggesting that cholinergic–sympathetic crosstalk can recruit vasodilator pathways under some experimental conditions (Wu et al., 2014). Thus, the net effect of SCG activation on cerebral perfusion depends on the balance between constrictor and dilator systems and on the local vascular segment studied.

## 4.3 Controversy over tonic SCG influence on resting cerebral blood flow

A longstanding question is whether the SCG exerts meaningful tonic control over resting cerebral blood flow. Many studies have found that resting cerebral blood flow is largely unchanged after cervical sympathectomy, and the SCG appears to have little influence on baseline cerebral autoregulation. In newborn lambs, cerebral autoregulation was minimally influenced by the superior cervical ganglion in two-week-old animals, and autoregulation was absent in preterm lambs immediately after delivery (Czynski et al., 2013). These developmental data suggest that SCG-dependent vasoconstriction is not essential for maintaining resting flow when perfusion pressure is stable.

However, the same pathway becomes functionally important during physiological stress. In awake rabbits, sympathetic nerves reduced cerebral blood flow during hypoxia, supporting the concept that SCG-mediated vasoconstriction protects the brain from excessive perfusion when arterial oxygen tension falls or when sympathetic activity is increased (Busija, 1984). A widely accepted interpretation is that the SCG is recruited as a protective brake on cerebral overperfusion during hypertension, hypoxia, or sympathetic surges, while contributing little to resting flow (Wahl & Schilling, 1993).

## 4.4 Regional and target-organ differences

The magnitude of SCG-dependent vascular control differs among cranial vascular beds. For example, bilateral superior cervical ganglionectomy increases choroidal blood flow in the rabbit, indicating that the SCG provides tonic vasoconstrictor restraint to the ocular circulation (Chou et al., 2000). By comparison, the cerebral circulation appears less dependent on tonic SCG activity under resting conditions. These regional differences may reflect the density of sympathetic innervation, the relative abundance of adrenoceptors and co-transmitters, and the capacity of local metabolic or myogenic mechanisms to offset sympathetic constriction. Therefore, findings from the eye or other cranial targets should not be extrapolated directly to the brain.

## 4.5 Summary and knowledge gaps

The SCG provides a powerful sympathetic constrictor pathway to the proximal cerebral circulation, but its physiological role is conditional rather than tonic. Resting cerebral blood flow and autoregulation are minimally affected by SCG removal in healthy or developing animals, whereas sympathetic activation during hypoxia, hypertension, or high sympathetic drive can significantly reduce cerebral perfusion. Important gaps remain, including the precise contribution of non-noradrenergic co-transmitters, the degree of segment-specific vascular control, and the transition from protective sympathetic restraint to harmful cerebral hypoperfusion in disease. These issues are particularly relevant to stroke, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and hypertensive cerebrovascular disease, which are addressed in the following section.
