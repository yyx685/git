# Section 6 — English Draft

**Section title:**  
The superior cervical ganglion as a neuro–immune–endocrine interface

---

## 6.1 SCG neurons and local immune regulation

Although the superior cervical ganglion (SCG) is best known for vasomotor control, it also communicates bidirectionally with immune cells. Co-culture studies have shown that SCG neurons can induce the differentiation of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells through calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), indicating that ganglionic neurons may directly shape local immune tolerance (Szklany et al., 2016). CGRP and vascular endothelial growth factor mRNAs are already expressed in the mouse SCG during pre- and postnatal development, suggesting that neuroimmune signalling is established early and may support both vascular and immune maturation in SCG target territories (Mitsuoka et al., 2018). Following peripheral nerve injury, molecular and cellular immune responses are recruited within peripheral ganglia, and the SCG participates in these injury-associated neuroimmune changes (Lindborg et al., 2018). These observations support the view that the SCG is not merely a passive conduit for sympathetic outflow but an active participant in local immune regulation.

## 6.2 Pineal innervation, melatonin, and sleep

The SCG provides the final sympathetic pathway to the pineal gland and is essential for the nocturnal synthesis of melatonin. The anatomical and physiological organisation of this pathway has been characterised in detail: noradrenergic postganglionic fibres from the SCG release norepinephrine onto pinealocytes, driving the enzymatic machinery responsible for melatonin production (Cardinali & Vacas, 1987; Moore, 1996; Møller & Baeres, 2002). This SCG–pineal axis links sympathetic activity to circadian rhythm, sleep, and endocrine homeostasis.

Recent evidence demonstrates that this pathway is also a target of immune attack. In a mouse model of cardiac disease, immune-mediated denervation of the pineal gland was identified as a mechanism underlying sleep disturbance, with loss of SCG-derived pineal innervation impairing melatonin signalling (Ziegler et al., 2023). These findings connect SCG dysfunction to the neuroendocrine and behavioural consequences of cardiovascular disease and suggest that preserving SCG–pineal connectivity may be important for sleep and recovery after cardiac or cerebrovascular events.

## 6.3 Inflammation, cytokines, and pain modulation

Cytokines can feed back onto SCG neurons and alter their excitability. TNF-α enhances the activity of sympathetic neurons in the SCG and promotes chronic sleep deprivation-related hyperalgesia, providing a direct link between inflammation, SCG hyperexcitability, and pain facilitation (Wu et al., 2025). This mechanism may be relevant to the autonomic and pain-related symptoms that accompany sleep loss, chronic stress, and systemic inflammation. It also complements the electrophysiological evidence in earlier sections that SCG neurons become hyperexcitable in disease, by identifying immune mediators as upstream drivers of that hyperexcitability.

## 6.4 Lymph node-like remodelling in disease

Under pathological conditions, the SCG can undergo striking structural remodelling. In rats after myocardial infarction, the left SCG exhibited lymph node-like features, including immune cell infiltration and architectural reorganisation, and this remodelling was associated with ventricular arrhythmias (Yu et al., 2025). This phenomenon blurs the traditional boundary between an autonomic ganglion and a secondary lymphoid organ, and it raises the possibility that remodelled SCG may serve as a local site of neuroimmune interaction during cardiovascular disease. Whether similar lymph node-like changes occur after stroke or subarachnoid hemorrhage is not yet known, but the finding has clear implications for cerebrovascular research.

## 6.5 Satellite glial cells and ganglionic injury responses

Within the SCG, satellite glial cells surround neuronal somata and respond rapidly to injury. After sympathetic nerve damage, satellite glial cells become activated, undergo structural changes, and express inflammatory markers, indicating that glial–neuronal signalling participates in the ganglionic response to axonal injury (Feldman-Goriachnik & Hanani, 2019). These glial responses may contribute to chronic pain, sympathetic sprouting, and persistent changes in ganglionic function. Because satellite glial cells are increasingly recognised as modulators of neuronal excitability, they represent an underexplored component of SCG-centred neuroimmune signalling.

## 6.6 Summary and knowledge gaps

The SCG functions as a neuro–immune–endocrine interface through at least four mechanisms: direct neuronal regulation of T-cell differentiation; sympathetic control of pineal melatonin synthesis; cytokine-mediated modulation of SCG excitability; and structural remodelling that brings immune cells into close contact with ganglionic neurons and glia. Major gaps remain. The relative importance of these pathways in cerebrovascular disease is largely unexplored, the molecular signals that trigger lymph node-like SCG remodelling are unknown, and it is unclear whether targeting SCG neuroimmune signalling could improve sleep, inflammation, or recovery after stroke. These questions link SCG biology to broader concepts of autonomic immunology and neuroendocrine control.
