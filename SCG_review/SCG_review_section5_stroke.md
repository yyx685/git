# Section 5 — English Draft

**Section title:**  
The superior cervical ganglion in stroke and cerebrovascular disease

---

## 5.1 Sympathetic overactivation and ischemic stroke

Ischemic stroke is accompanied by a robust autonomic stress response in which sympathetic outflow increases acutely. Because the superior cervical ganglion (SCG) is a major relay for sympathetic input to the proximal cerebral arteries, its excessive activation has the potential to reduce cerebral perfusion precisely when the ischemic penumbra is most vulnerable. Electrical stimulation of the SCG produces potent cerebral vasoconstriction, and sympathetic hyperactivity has been shown to cause marked cerebral hypoperfusion in large-animal models (Kim et al., 2022a; Kim et al., 2022b). These observations support the concept that pathological SCG-mediated vasoconstriction can aggravate flow reduction in cerebrovascular disease.

Experimental interruption of perivascular sympathetic nerves has provided functional evidence for this hypothesis. Sympathetic denervation of cerebral arteries confers neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia, suggesting that restraining SCG-derived sympathetic tone may preserve collateral or residual perfusion and limit infarct progression (Lee et al., 2017). Conversely, impaired sympathetic restraint has been implicated in abnormal regional cerebral blood flow responses during hypotension in stroke-prone spontaneously hypertensive rats, indicating that the balance between protective and harmful effects of SCG activity is altered in hypertensive cerebrovascular disease (Sadoshima & Heistad, 1983). Thus, the SCG appears to be a dual-edged regulator: its activity may be protective under physiological stress but harmful when exaggerated or dysregulated after stroke.

## 5.2 Subarachnoid hemorrhage and cerebral vasospasm

Cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage remains a major cause of delayed cerebral ischemia. Sympathetic mechanisms have long been proposed to contribute to vasospasm because the large proximal cerebral arteries that are most prone to spasm are densely innervated by SCG fibres. Early clinical and experimental work suggested that sympathetic block could relieve vasospasm (Day & Theodosopoulos, 2003). More recently, cervical sympathectomy was shown to modulate neurogenic inflammatory neuropeptides following experimental subarachnoid hemorrhage, providing a mechanistic link between SCG activity, neuropeptide release, and vascular inflammation (Dagistan et al., 2019). In addition, experimental degeneration of the superior cervical ganglion was associated with prevention of basilar artery spasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage, further supporting a causal role of SCG output in post-hemorrhagic vasospasm (Sahin et al., 2022).

These mechanistic findings have been translated into novel intervention strategies. An endovascular transmural technique for pharmacological blockade of the SCG prevented sympathetic-mediated cerebral vasospasm in a preclinical model, offering a more selective alternative to surgical ganglionectomy or systemic sympathetic blockade (Kim et al., 2024). Recurrent cervical internal carotid artery vasospasm has also been described as a clinical entity with vessel wall imaging and endovascular management, underscoring the need for better anatomical targeting of cervical sympathetic structures (Tokunaga et al., 2023). These developments position the SCG as an actionable target for preventing or treating cerebral vasospasm.

## 5.3 Hypertension and cerebrovascular remodelling

Chronic hypertension is a major risk factor for stroke and is characterised by increased sympathetic drive and structural remodelling of cerebral arteries. The SCG is both a source of sympathetic innervation and a site of hypertension-induced plasticity. In spontaneously hypertensive rats, superior cervical ganglionectomy attenuated vascular remodelling, indicating that SCG-derived sympathetic signalling actively promotes structural changes in the resistance vasculature during hypertension (Wang et al., 2025). This finding extends the role of the SCG from acute vasoconstriction to chronic vascular injury and remodelling.

The clinical relevance of this observation is twofold. First, prolonged SCG overactivity may contribute to the hypertensive cerebrovascular phenotype that predisposes to lacunar infarction and intracerebral hemorrhage. Second, interventions that reduce SCG output may have disease-modifying effects beyond immediate blood pressure reduction, although the translational window and side effects of chronic SCG interruption remain to be defined.

## 5.4 SCG as a therapeutic target in cerebrovascular disease

Taken together, the evidence suggests that the SCG represents a promising node for interrupting sympathetic-mediated cerebrovascular injury. Potential strategies include ultrasound-guided SCG block, radiofrequency lesioning, surgical ganglionectomy, and endovascular transmural pharmacological block. Each approach differs in selectivity, reversibility, and risk of Horner syndrome or other autonomic complications. Preclinical studies indicate that SCG blockade can reduce cerebral vasospasm and protect against ischemic injury, but most human data remain limited to case series, technical reports, or extrapolation from stellate ganglion block. Controlled studies are needed to determine whether SCG-targeted intervention improves functional outcome after stroke or subarachnoid hemorrhage.

## 5.5 Summary and knowledge gaps

The SCG contributes to cerebrovascular pathophysiology through acute sympathetic vasoconstriction, neurogenic inflammation, post-hemorrhagic vasospasm, and chronic hypertension-induced vascular remodelling. Although experimental studies consistently support a role for SCG signalling in these processes, several questions remain unresolved. It is unclear when SCG activity is protective and when it becomes harmful after stroke; whether the benefit of SCG interruption is mediated mainly by improved perfusion, reduced inflammation, or prevention of remodelling; and whether selective SCG blockade is superior to broader sympathetic inhibition. Addressing these questions will require integrated haemodynamic, molecular, and imaging studies in clinically relevant stroke models.
