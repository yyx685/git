# Section 2 — English Draft

**Section title:**  
Anatomy and neurochemistry of the superior cervical ganglion–cerebrovascular axis

---

## 2.1 Gross anatomy and segmental connectivity

The superior cervical ganglion (SCG) is the most rostral ganglion of the paravertebral sympathetic chain and is typically described as a fusiform structure lying ventral to the transverse processes of C1–C3 and anterior to the longus capitis muscle. It receives preganglionic sympathetic fibres that ascend within the cervical sympathetic trunk from neurons located in the upper thoracic spinal cord, and it distributes postganglionic fibres through gray rami communicantes to the upper cervical spinal nerves and through named branches to the internal and external carotid arteries, pharyngeal region, and adjacent structures. Morphological studies in Japanese cadaver donors have shown that the SCG is intimately related to the C1–C4 cervical nerves, and that individual nerve communications vary considerably between specimens (Mitsuoka et al., 2017). This anatomical variability has practical implications for surgical denervation, image-guided block, and the interpretation of studies using superior cervical ganglionectomy.

The traditional view that the SCG functions only as a simple relay station has been challenged by tracing experiments. In the rat, the SCG contains not only synaptic inputs onto its principal neurons but also axons of passage that traverse the ganglion without synapsing, and it receives additional inputs beyond the classic cervical sympathetic trunk (Wang et al., 2018). These observations suggest that the SCG may integrate multiple ascending and local signals rather than merely transmit a uniform sympathetic command.

## 2.2 Projection pathways to the cerebral circulation

Postganglionic fibres arising from the SCG ascend with the internal carotid nerve and form the internal carotid plexus. From this plexus, sympathetic fibres continue into the cavernous segment of the internal carotid artery and then distribute to the proximal segments of the anterior cerebral, middle cerebral, posterior communicating, and posterior cerebral arteries, as well as to the rostral portion of the basilar artery. Electron microscopic and physiological studies established that intracranial arteries receive sympathetic nerve terminals from the SCG, although the density is highest in large proximal vessels and decreases toward smaller pial arteries (Sato et al., 1980; Marín et al., 1980).

An important feature of this projection is its bilaterality. Bilateral innervation of the cerebral arteries by the SCG has been demonstrated in cats, indicating that unilateral lesion or blockade may not completely abolish sympathetic input to a given vascular territory (Marín et al., 1980). This finding helps to explain why the cerebrovascular consequences of unilateral SCG intervention are often partial or variable. In addition, the human cervical vagus nerve contains sympathetic fibres, some of which may represent collateral or accessory sympathetic pathways relevant to cervical vagus nerve stimulation (Ruigrok et al., 2023). Therefore, the SCG should be regarded as the major, but not necessarily the exclusive, source of sympathetic fibres reaching the proximal cerebral circulation.

## 2.3 Neurochemical phenotype of SCG neurons

Norepinephrine is the principal classical transmitter of postganglionic SCG neurons, and noradrenergic synaptic transmission is a defining feature of this ganglion (Kawai, 1999). However, SCG neurons are chemically heterogeneous. Neuropeptide Y is frequently co-localized with norepinephrine in sympathetic perivascular fibres, where it can contribute to prolonged vasoconstriction and modulate noradrenergic transmission. In the human SCG, immunohistochemical studies have identified several additional neuropeptides, including vasoactive intestinal peptide, calcitonin gene-related peptide, and enkephalin, although their cellular sources and functional roles are not fully resolved (Baffi et al., 1992). Peptide receptors are also expressed within the human SCG, suggesting that neuropeptide signalling may act locally within the ganglion as well as at postganglionic terminals (Tajti et al., 1999).

This neurochemical complexity has two important implications. First, the cerebrovascular effects of SCG activation cannot be attributed solely to α-adrenergic stimulation, because co-released neuropeptides and other mediators may produce delayed or long-lasting vascular actions. Second, the expression of neuropeptides and their receptors may be altered by injury, hypertension, or ageing, thereby changing the net output of the SCG to cerebral vessels under pathological conditions.

## 2.4 Anatomical variations, imaging, and methodological considerations

From a clinical and translational perspective, the SCG is increasingly targeted by ultrasound-guided block, radiofrequency ablation, and emerging endovascular transmural approaches. Accurate anatomical localization is therefore critical. Magnetic resonance imaging can identify the SCG and the inferior ganglion of the vagus nerve, which are important because they can mimic pathologic retropharyngeal lymph nodes (Yokota et al., 2018). Recognition of this pitfall is relevant both for diagnostic imaging and for planning image-guided interventions.

Methodologically, animal models of SCG manipulation require careful surgical standardization. Detailed protocols for superior cervical ganglionectomy and decentralization have been described in rats and mice, and they emphasize complete removal of the ganglion, avoidance of injury to adjacent structures, and verification of denervation (Madhani et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). Differences in surgical completeness, laterality, and the age of animals are likely sources of variability among studies examining the cerebrovascular or metabolic consequences of SCG removal.

## 2.5 Summary and knowledge gaps

The SCG is anatomically positioned to provide the dominant sympathetic innervation of the proximal cerebral circulation, and its neurons are neurochemically diverse rather than purely noradrenergic. Key remaining questions include the precise contribution of non-noradrenergic co-transmitters to cerebral vascular control, the extent and functional significance of bilateral and accessory sympathetic pathways, and the degree to which SCG anatomical variation in humans influences the outcome of SCG-targeted interventions. These anatomical and neurochemical considerations provide the basis for understanding SCG physiology and its contribution to cerebrovascular disease in the following sections.
